The Likelihood of Suicidal Ideation in Mass Shooters - A Criminological Profile of+ the Buffalo Tops Supermarket Shooter and Strain Theory

Jacob Moody — academic criminology paper

A responsible-reporting note: this is academic research applying Robert Agnew’s General Strain Theory to the structural risk factors behind mass violence and suicidality — strain, isolation, blocked opportunity — in order to understand and help prevent harm. It contains no methods, no operational detail, and no glorification, and follows safe-messaging practice. If this subject is heavy for you right now, please use the resources above.

Over the last two decades, the United States has seen a troubling overlap between highly publicized acts of mass violence and growing concern about suicide and self-destructive behavior. From the civil unrest of the 1990s to the shock of 9/11 and the long “War on Terror,” political polarization and social instability have created a sense that the world is increasingly unsafe and out of control. Criminologist Robert Agnew’s General Strain Theory (GST) helps explain why this matters: when people are exposed to intense and repeated strains—such as poverty, rural isolation, bureaucratic rejection, or blocked opportunities for success—they experience mounting pressure that can fuel anger, despair, and deviant coping. In this context, constant media coverage does not just inform the public; it can amplify fear, normalize extreme narratives, and accelerate the cycle of violence. This paper argues that the 2022 Buffalo Tops supermarket shooting can be understood not only as a hate crime, but also as a form of outwardly directed suicide: an act in which the offender’s suicidal despair and sense of strain were projected onto others, and which can be meaningfully analyzed through General Strain Theory. On May 14, 2022, eighteen-year-old Payton Gendron opened fire at a Tops supermarket in Buffalo, New York. Armed with a legally purchased AR-15 style rifle and wearing body armor, he killed ten Black victims and injured three others, ranging in age from twenty to eighty-six. When confronted by police, Gendron surrendered at the scene and later stated that he was motivated by a conspiracy belief that white people were being “replaced” and stripped of power.

Gendron’s stated motive connects directly to core concepts in General Strain Theory. GST emphasizes that anger and violence often emerge when individuals experience the failure to achieve valued goals or the removal of positive stimuli. For Gendron, the perceived “threat” of replacement functioned as the imagined loss of status and dominance—a symbolic removal of something he believed he was entitled to. Over time, this type of strain can distort a person’s perception of reality and erode judgment. Combined with social segregation and racial hierarchy, these beliefs can harden into a worldview where violent action feels like the only way to respond.

The violence in Buffalo was not an isolated explosion of hatred; it emerged from an environment that reinforced and amplified Gendron’s beliefs. Radical online communities and certain local attitudes can normalize deviance by framing racist conspiracy theories as logical or even heroic. This parallels the psychological profile of offenders like Ted Kaczynski, who claimed a moral high ground while using violence to “send a message.” In Gendron’s case, his digital and social ecosystem kept his ideology alive and intensified, while national media coverage of previous attacks provided a script for how to act.

Critically, the internal experience of many mass shooters is one of desperation, not simple cold-blooded calculation. Lankford et al. (2021) found that mass shooters frequently exhibit suicidal ideation and often plan their attacks with the expectation that they will die. In that sense, the attack becomes a form of outwardly directed suicide: the offender lashes out like a cornered animal, externalizing their pain onto others while assuming they are about to die. By imagining themselves as martyrs rather than criminals, they try to regain a final sense of control and status in a life they perceive as already lost.

The Buffalo shooting demonstrates that without proper mental health advocacy and exposure to diverse perspectives this problem will worsen if justice policy shift from purely reactive punishment to addressing the root strain, the cycle can be disrupted. This requires putting effort into behavioral health and community outreach rather than broad and effective bans. Research by Brucato et el. (2021) Suggests that policies focus solely on mental illness in the broad sense have limited impact as most mass shooters do not suffer from psychosis instead policies must target specific behavioral risks such as the leakage of suicidal intent and the isolation that breeds extremism all signs point to a gradual but exponential rate of societal healing if we remove the bias and address the problem as a whole treating the mass shooting not just as a crime of hate but as a fatal outwardly directed crisis of despair.

Works Cited

Brucato, G., Appelbaum, P. S., Hesson, H., Shea, E. A., Dishy, G., Lee, K., Pia, T., Syed, F., Villalobos, A., Wall, M. M., Lieberman, J. A., & Girgis, R. R. (2021). Psychotic symptoms in mass shootings v. mass murders not involving firearms: Findings from the columbia mass murder database. Psychological Medicine, 52(15), 1–9. https://doi.org/10.1017/s0033291721000076. In this study, the authors find that although mass shootings account for only a fraction of violent crimes, they disproportionately affect society due to stigma and misrepresentation—suggesting the prevalence is a public-health concern. The authors examined records from 1900–2019, reviewing 14,785 publicly described cases that included perpetrator demographics, legal history, substance use, mental-health history, and firearm type (semi-automatic vs. automatic). Results identified lifelong psychotic symptoms in 11% of perpetrators—consistent with older reports—including 18% of mass murderers who did not use firearms. Of the 1,315 mass murders, 65% involved firearms. The study finds that U.S.-based mass shooters were more likely to have legal histories, drug and alcohol use, or non-psychotic or neurological symptoms. The authors suggest that policies focused solely on mental illness have limited impact, whereas policies addressing firearm access, legal history, and mental-health advocacy may be more effective.

Lankford, A., Silver, J., & Cox, J. (2021). An epidemiological analysis of public mass shooters and active shooters: Quantifying key differences between perpetrators and the general population, homicide offenders, and people who die by suicide. Journal of Threat Assessment and Management, 11(1). https://doi.org/10.1037/tam0000166. This study compares 171 U.S Public mass shooters and 63 active shooter cases with the general population, homicide offenders, and those who die by suicide. It finds that mass shooters more closely resemble those that commit suicide than typical homicide offenders. The data shows the shooters are more likely to act alone, to plan the act, suicidal ideation and to die from the event (either self inflicted or “suicide by law enforcement”. To contrast these findings, the study found that prior felony convictions and pre-existing firearm ownership did not significantly differ from the general population. The authors Lankford, Silver and Cox argue this suggests that traditional threat assessment measures like criminal history or gun ownership may actually only be of limited predictive value and instead; suicide-prevention strategies and interventions that focus isolation, planning and self-destructive tendencies may be more effective in reducing risk of mass public shootings..
