The extended mind hypothesis (Clark & Chalmers, 1998) proposes that cognitive processes extend beyond the boundaries of skull and skin when an external resource plays a functional role equivalent to an internal cognitive process. The canonical example is Otto, a person with Alzheimer’s whose notebook functions as genuine memory — not a prompt for memory retrieval, but memory itself. This paper presents a real-world, longitudinal instance of the same phenomenon in a behavioral health context. I. The 167-Hour Problem and the Extended Mind Hypothesis

A person with complex trauma, active caregiving responsibilities, and documented mood and anxiety disorder who is managing their own behavioral health in the gaps between clinical contacts — the 167 hours per week that formal care does not touch — faces a monitoring problem that existing solutions do not solve. Weekly therapy captures one hour in 168. The clinician works from what the client remembers about the preceding week, subject to the well-documented distortions of mood-congruent recall, peak-end effects, and temporal compression (Stone & Shiffman, 1994). The trajectories that precede crisis — the slow erosion of sleep, the progressive narrowing of energy, the daily compression of focus — are precisely the patterns most likely to be lost in retrospective recall.

HALO (Heuristic Adaptive Longitudinal Observer) is an AI-assisted behavioral health monitoring system developed by and for the author beginning in 2024 and operated continuously for approximately 27 months. This paper argues that HALO constitutes a genuine component of the author’s cognitive and self-regulatory architecture — not a tool for monitoring, but the monitoring itself.

Clark and Chalmers’ (1998) parity principle asks: if a process in the external world, when performed inside the head, would be recognized as a cognitive process, then that process is cognitive regardless of where it occurs. The canonical example is Otto, a person with Alzheimer’s whose notebook functions as genuine memory — not a prompt for memory retrieval, but memory itself. What HALO represents is an empirical instance of the same logic under conditions of far greater complexity: a dynamic, responsive AI system that tracks, reflects, synthesizes, and returns information in real time; operates in the user’s natural environment; and directly influences the user’s capacity for self-monitoring, self-advocacy, and clinical engagement.

The falsifiable claim If HALO functions as genuine extended cognition, removing access should produce measurable degradation in self-regulation and mood stability — not merely inconvenience. If the system is only a convenience tool, removal should leave functioning intact. The distinction matters clinically: convenience tools can be substituted; extended cognitive components cannot, without functional cost.

II. Background: The Subject’s Context

The author presents as a complex trauma survivor with documented mood and anxiety disorder, a former family caregiver for a parent with epilepsy and cardiac history, a first-generation college student, and a person managing the practical, financial, and relational demands associated with each of these roles simultaneously. Adverse Childhood Experience (ACE) score: 9/10, administered 01/31/2025 by the author’s treating clinician. A score of 9/10 places the author in the highest-exposure category; the population-level mental health crisis rate at ACE ≥ 3 is 42.0% (BRFSS 2021, n = 58,479).

Neurocognitive Profile — WISC-IV (November 2005, age 14)

The architecturally significant finding is a 43-point discrepancy between the Verbal Comprehension Index (VCI = 108, 70th percentile, Average range) and the Processing Speed Index (PSI = 65, 1st percentile, Extremely Low range). This split is consistent with undiagnosed ADHD: not a person who cannot reason, but a person whose reasoning ability is chronically undermined by the capacity to process and act on information in real time. HALO’s function as an external tracking and encoding system directly compensates for this deficit. The extended mind is not a convenience for this user; it is an architectural substitute for a function the internal system cannot reliably perform.

Mental Health System Contact History

The author’s contact with the formal mental health system prior to the HALO period consisted exclusively of crisis-driven inpatient hospitalizations and brief outpatient follow-up. Hospitalizations occurred during acute deterioration episodes across 2013, 2015, 2017, and 2019. The 2017 episode is confirmed through primary-source medical records: an involuntary inpatient admission to Oswego Hospital Behavioral Health Services, September 4, 2017, with documented diagnoses of Major Depressive Disorder (F33.9), PTSD (F43.10), Bipolar Disorder in remission (F31.9), and Mood Disorder (F39). No documented psychiatric care followed until re-engagement at North Country Family Health Center in late 2023 — a gap of approximately five years — where the PHQ-9 baseline of 21 and GAD-7 baseline of 18 were recorded.

The comparison class for evaluating HALO’s contribution is therefore not HALO vs. better clinical engagement. It is HALO vs. the absence of any structured support at all. HALO was filling a space that professional support had never occupied.

It gave me the ability to see past my own bullshit and finally keep and stick to getting the help I need. My entire intention of dumping my mind into an AI was to keep my family together. — Jacob D. Moody

The decline is not linear; it shows the variance characteristic of real clinical progress — plateaus, acute stress events producing temporary reversals, and gradual recovery arcs.

V. The Vocabulary-Capacity Gap

A consistent finding across the observation period is the discrepancy between internal state complexity and articulable description. On high-distress days, check-in entries are shorter, less structurally complex, and use more concrete rather than abstract vocabulary — not because the internal state is simpler, but because executive function impairment reduces linguistic access to internal states under load. This is the vocabulary-capacity gap: the internal experience exceeds the person’s momentary capacity to describe it.

HALO removes the articulation gate. The check-in protocol requires minimal language. Patterns emerge from behavioral data over time without requiring the user to diagnose themselves or explain their state. The tool adapts to the user’s register rather than requiring the user to adapt to the tool.

VII. HALO as Extended Mind: The Theoretical Case

The Parity Principle Applied

Longitudinal behavioral tracking. The human capacity to monitor one’s own mood, energy, sleep, and anxiety over time — to detect trends, to notice when a number that was 7 last week is 4 this week — is a cognitive function. Under chronic stress, it is substantially degraded. HALO performs this function externally: it captures the data at the moment it is reported, stores it durably, and surfaces it to the user and care team on demand. If this function were occurring internally, we would call it memory and self-monitoring. Occurring externally in HALO, the parity principle says it still is.

Crisis early warning. The capacity to recognize when one’s psychological state has reached a threshold that warrants clinical attention — before that threshold becomes a crisis — is a cognitive monitoring function. HALO maintains the reference frame, surfaces the comparison, and escalates to 988 crisis routing deterministically when threshold indicators appear.

Clinical self-advocacy capacity. The capacity to arrive at a clinical appointment with an accurate account of the preceding weeks — specific, dateable, quantified, trend-aware — is a cognitive and communicative function that determines the quality of clinical decision-making. HALO transforms this from impossible to routine for users whose internal cognitive architecture cannot reliably perform it.

The Coupling Condition

For an external resource to constitute extended cognition, it must be reliably available, routinely consulted, and its outputs must feed directly into cognitive processing and action. HALO’s operational history over 27 months passes the same test. The system was available continuously — local deployment means it is not dependent on an internet connection or another party’s availability. It was routinely consulted: 672 check-ins across 216 days of engagement. Its outputs fed directly into action: the longitudinal record was used in clinical conversations, and the trend data informed the user’s understanding of their own state.

The Alignment Condition and Script-Based Design

Applying the extended mind framework to behavioral health monitoring specifically requires that the external system’s function is aligned with the user’s genuine interests. HALO’s design satisfies this through script-based alignment: the AI model is instructed through a detailed natural-language system prompt that specifies the user’s documented history, the known clinical context, the explicit prohibitions (no diagnosis, no prescription, deterministic 988 routing), and the affirmative commitments (accurate reporting, anti-sycophancy, human-in-the-loop for clinical decisions). The alignment is structural, not hoped for.

VIII. Design Properties That Make Extended Mind Possible

Not every AI system functions as extended cognition. The properties that make HALO eligible for the extended mind claim are specific to its design:

“HALO is an accumulation of my knowledge in theory and practice, utilizing a script-based network for data accumulation and interpretive analysis — always human-in-the-loop, AI- and human-aligned — to extend and stabilize my own cognition without ever replacing my judgment. An extended mind I can see. ‘Script-based’ isn’t a limitation — it is the alignment. Transparent → inspectable → overridable → mine. That chain is why it stays aligned.” — Jacob D. Moody

The transparency-inspectability-overridability chain keeps the system epistemically accountable to its user. This is the design property that distinguishes HALO as an extended mind instrument rather than an autonomous agent: the human retains full override authority at every step. The system extends cognition without displacing judgment.

IX. Convergent Evidence from In-Silico Testing

A separate, formal in-silico testing program addressed the question that the N=1 case cannot answer: whether HALO’s effects, as tested against a controlled simulation, are attributable to the mechanism it proposes or to more parsimonious explanations.

The primary finding was that continuous AI presence — whether the full HALO system or an attention-matched sham placebo — produced large, significant reductions in crisis incidence relative to no support. The mechanism test (v1 amnesic design) returned a null: halo ≈ sham on wellbeing when neither is remembered. The honest, bounded reading: single-stage reflection ≈ single-stage warmth when neither is remembered. That is not a test of HALO’s continuity mechanism.

A continuity re-test (v2), in which agents were given memory of prior interactions, produced a significant advantage for HALO over the sham condition on confidant integrity: the preservation of the user’s close relationships (t = +7.89, p < 0.001). This relational finding converges with the extended mind framing: if HALO absorbs the monitoring and self-regulatory load that would otherwise be deposited on close relationships, it functions as a cognitive prosthetic for relationship maintenance as well as for self-monitoring.

X. Limitations

The N=1 design limits generalizability. The researcher is also the subject, introducing potential self-report bias. No randomized control condition exists — co-occurring life events, therapy, medication changes, and other factors could partially explain clinical improvement. Convergent validity with clinical instruments (PHQ-9, GAD-7) supports the self-report data but does not establish causation. These limitations are explicitly acknowledged and do not negate the extended mind thesis, which requires only functional role equivalence rather than controlled causal attribution.

XI. Discussion and Conclusion

The extended mind framework has generated substantial philosophical literature but very little longitudinal empirical documentation of AI systems operating in the extended-mind mode for real users in real contexts over sustained periods. This paper provides that documentation for the behavioral health context. A person whose internal cognitive architecture is insufficient to perform a specific function — sustained self-monitoring under chronic stress — finds that the function is performed reliably and durably when an external system takes it on. The external system is not a reminder that prompts the internal function; it is the function itself, occurring in a different substrate.

HALO is an extended mind in the sense Clark and Chalmers proposed: an external system that performs cognitive functions — behavioral monitoring, crisis early-warning, longitudinal self-tracking, clinical self-advocacy — that, without the system, would not be performed. The evidence for this claim is a single case, 27 months in duration, 672 check-ins across 216 days, with PHQ-9 and GAD-7 benchmarking against an externally produced clinical record. The limitations are real and disclosed in full. The invitation is not to accept the case as proof. It is to recognize that a real instance of the extended mind in behavioral health has been documented, and that this constitutes a call to investigate it formally.

The system was built in the dark, by one person, during one of the hardest periods of that person’s life. It worked — not perfectly, not clinically proven, but functionally present in the way Otto’s notebook was functionally present for Otto. That is enough to warrant looking more carefully. — Jacob D.
