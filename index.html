Precedent Is Not Principle

An American Opinion on the War in Iran

This essay is the culmination of a thesis I have been building across four semesters of coursework at Jefferson Community College — through philosophy, criminology, literature, art history, and western civilization — and months of independent geopolitical analysis. It draws on and cites my prior academic work where relevant, including papers on American immigration policy (2024), media framing of violence (2025), the behavioral patterns of mass shooters (2025), Roman class warfare (2025), post-colonial identity (2025), medieval propaganda (2025), and the moral obligations of scholars (2026). The argument is not new. It has been building, and recent events have made it impossible to stay quiet.

I. What Was Said — and What Wasn’t

On March 13th, a weekly faculty seminar was held on the current war in the Middle East. The presentation was thorough. It covered Sun Tzu, Vietnam, the War Powers Resolution of 1973, Clausewitz’s framework of war as a continuation of politics. It traced the degradation of American military objectives from the total victory of World War II through the undefined engagements of Korea, Vietnam, the War on Terror, and now Iran. It noted Dresden, Cambodia, and Laos. It acknowledged the Sunni-Shia divide and the failed expectation of an independent Kurdistan — the Kurds who ultimately backed out because they saw where it was heading. It even compared America to Athens and referenced Prussia’s attacks born of survival. The history was solid. The points that were made were true.

But what wasn’t said is what concerns me. Presidential precedent for bypassing the War Powers Resolution was presented as though precedent itself were justification. Every president has done it. Therefore it is done. That framing — regardless of intent — trickles down. When an institution walks a room full of students through a pattern without naming it as a pattern, the takeaway is that the pattern is normal. If every president since 1973 has bypassed the legislative check designed to prevent exactly the kind of unilateral military engagement we are now witnessing in Iran, then the mechanism has failed. The pattern is the problem. And nobody in the room said so.

One question cut through it. A voice from the audience asked: has the United States ever not been at war? The answer is that we haven’t had a peaceful stretch since the 1950s in which we weren’t engaged in armed conflict without a clearly defined objective. That question should have reframed the entire discussion. It didn’t. And that is not a criticism of the presenter — it is an observation about the trickle-down effect of what happens when reason lapses and we accept the pattern as inevitable rather than examining why it keeps repeating.

There were students in that audience asking about the possibility of drone attacks on American soil. Kids. Asking with genuine fear. And the room couldn’t give them a straight answer. That should tell you everything about where we are.

II. “Topple Iran” Is Not an Objective

The seminar asked the right question: what is a clear objective? Without one, a war is pointless and lost before it starts. It addressed the realities on the ground — our dwindling munitions supply in the area of operations, the question of how we define success, and the hard truth that strategy wins wars. Strategic victory is total victory, the seminar explained, and that is correct. We cannot say for certain what the outcomes of this current conflict will be. But we can say with certainty that “topple Iran” is not a strategic objective. It is a slogan. What follows the toppling? We couldn’t answer that question in Iraq. We couldn’t answer it in Afghanistan. We couldn’t answer it in Libya. The absence of a post-conflict strategy is not a gap in planning — it is the pattern itself. We break things. We leave. The vacuum fills with something worse, and a decade later we act surprised.

The seminar glossed over the fact that the United States funded the entirety of the South Vietnamese government’s war effort until the Paris Peace Accords in 1973. Vietnam was touched on as a reference point but not as a warning. Vietnam is not an anecdote. It is the template. We defined success after Vietnam as winning battles and killing Vietnamese. But during the war, the stated purpose was fighting communism and evil. The objective shifted underneath us because it was never fixed to begin with. An escalating commitment with no exit strategy, a War Powers Resolution born directly from the aftermath — and then fifty years of ignoring it.

The War on Terror compounded the problem. When the seminar raised the question of clear objectives, my mind went immediately to terrorism — and then I had to ask myself: what exactly is a terrorist? It has never been clearly defined. The label shifted to fit whoever needed to be the enemy next, and the authorization for use of military force became a blank check. Now we are in Iran, and the question isn’t whether we have the authority. The question is whether authority without objective is just violence with a flag on it.

The Strait of Hormuz came up in the seminar, and that situation is only going to get worse. Look at the escalatory behavior and posturing around the region. Iran’s neighbors aren’t doing anything to stop the onslaught, even if it means getting caught in the crossfire. That isn’t neutrality. That is malicious compliance. They are watching us exhaust ourselves and they are content to let it happen.

I’ve been tracking this longer than most people think. On December 19th, 2025 — the day I finished my fall semester finals — I sat down and laid out a six-to-eight month forecast for armed conflict escalation based on behavioral pattern analysis I had been building since at least November. I said at the time: “My prediction isn’t a prediction, it’s an inevitability at this point. The United States is going to escalate conflict to the point of a global conflict.” I was told to pump the brakes. Operation Epic Fury launched ten weeks later.

Since late February 2026, I’ve been applying the analytical methods I learned in these classrooms to publicly available news. I read nineteen international outlets — AP, Reuters, BBC, NPR, Al Jazeera, and others — and cross-reference their reporting to separate pattern from noise, the same way a research methods course teaches you to triangulate sources. On February 27th, based on the trajectory I was seeing, I wrote that the Iran situation would likely escalate within a month. It launched the next morning. By January, I had already been following Cuba’s energy grid collapse and the Venezuelan oil cutoff, connecting them as linked stressors months before they made mainstream headlines. By March 7th, I noticed a structural parallel between the Florida speedboat incident and the Gleiwitz incident of 1939 — both events that served as narrative anchors for escalation already in motion. None of this required classified intelligence or special access. It required reading widely, thinking historically, and being willing to connect dots across disciplines — which is supposed to be the point of a liberal arts education.

III. Both Can Be True

The points made in that room were true — and bluntly stated, which I respect. The counterpoints raised were also true. Both can be true at the same time. That is not a contradiction — it is the nature of history. The problem is that labeling theory, the same framework I will use later in this paper to describe American behavior abroad, applies globally and in both directions.

We were taught that we are a beacon of hope. The shiniest of European experiments. And in many respects, that was earned. We did win our independence — not just because we kicked Britain’s ass, but because the colonies had become a financial drag. The tea tax was so high because it cost a fortune to feed British culture to the New World and still maintain it. The revolution was as much an economic inevitability as it was an ideological one. We won, and we built something genuinely new. That is true.

It is also true that what we built was an extension of what we inherited. Where did the Industrial Revolution start? England. We expanded on it here. There is a popular notion that whatever Europe makes, America perfects. Maybe that applies to manufacturing and technology. But the same cannot be said about culture. We inherited the imperial impulse along with the machinery, and we have been running that program ever since.

We forget, as Americans, that England supported the Confederacy — and the nature of that support is telling. Britain’s relationship with the Confederate States was a complex mix of official neutrality and unofficial support. The British government never formally recognized the CSA as a sovereign nation, but British industry and private citizens played a vital role in sustaining the Southern war effort. British shipyards built Confederate commerce raiders like the CSS Alabama. British textile mills, dependent on Southern cotton, had every financial incentive to see the Confederacy survive. British merchants ran the Union blockade to keep supply lines open. The government said one thing. The money did another. That is the template — say the right words, let the market do the dirty work, and maintain plausible deniability. We inherited that, too. European ambassadors observed this gigantic human experiment of democracy rip itself apart over states’ rights — but the right to do what? Own a person. And then we proceeded to try to make up for it by paying them less than minimum wage. Here is a fact that most people don’t know: in September 1942, FBI agents raided a farm in Beeville, Texas, and freed Alfred Irving — a cognitively disabled Black man who had been shackled, starved to eighty-five pounds, and beaten for years by his captors. It is widely considered the last federal prosecution of chattel-style slavery in the United States (Blackmon, 2008). The prosecution was only possible because Attorney General Francis Biddle had issued Circular No. 3591 five days after Pearl Harbor, directing federal prosecutors to aggressively pursue involuntary servitude cases — motivated in part by the need to counter Axis propaganda exploiting American racial hypocrisy during wartime (Daniel, 1972). When it boiled down to it, we couldn’t fight oppression overseas if we were technically still practicing it at home. But we still wouldn’t let Black soldiers serve alongside white units. Truman signed Executive Order 9981 in 1948, but full integration didn’t happen until Korea forced it — the last all-Black unit wasn’t disbanded until 1954 (National Archives).

And the interventionist impulse did not start with the twentieth century. By 1801 — barely a generation after independence — we were already projecting force overseas. The First Barbary War put American warships in Tripoli harbor. After the War of 1812 confirmed we could fight Britain to a standstill, the Monroe Doctrine declared the entire Western Hemisphere our sphere of influence. We annexed Texas, provoked a war with Mexico, took half their country, and called it manifest destiny. We forced Japan open at gunpoint in 1853. We took the Philippines, Puerto Rico, and Guam from Spain in 1898 and spent years putting down Filipino resistance. We occupied Haiti from 1915 to 1934. We have been intervening since before the ink dried on the Constitution, and the Civil War proved we would do it to ourselves if we ran out of foreign targets. The pattern is not a twentieth-century phenomenon. It is baked into the national identity.

These are the kinds of details that historians seem to forget in their hubris. Tiny behaviors — a policy here, a quiet exemption there, a segregated unit, a technical loophole — these are what drive the next generation’s civil conflict. And if left unmanaged, they spill into complete turmoil. Hate breeds hate. The resentment we generated abroad through decades of intervention is coming home in the form of the very wars we keep starting. The resentment we generated domestically through centuries of inequality is tearing us apart from the inside while we try to fight on three fronts. Both are true. Both are our doing.

There is a behavioral model that applies here, and it scales fractally from the individual to the nation-state: when identity is questioned, the subject doubles down. It works the same way whether you’re talking about a person in a therapy session or a country at a podium. The United States’ identity is being questioned — by its allies, by its adversaries, by its own citizens — and the response is not reflection. It is escalation. I’ve studied this pattern in behavioral health and I’ve watched it play out in geopolitics, and I can tell you: empires collapse for the same reasons people relapse. The mechanism is identical. The scale is the only difference.

Through all of this, there is a clear and present problem: administrations repeatedly not listening to the people they train to do jobs. Psychologists, analysts, regional experts — the people whose entire function is to understand behavior, risk, and consequence — are sidelined the moment their analysis becomes inconvenient. What no one likes about therapy is that we as humans have to look inward to exact the changes required to function as what gets called a “normal” human being. Nations are no different. The resistance to self-examination scales from the individual to the institution. We train people to understand human behavior and then refuse to listen when they tell us what the behavior means.

In a Western Civilization essay on the Roman Republic (Moody, 2025a), I traced how victorious Roman soldiers returned home from war to find their farms seized, sold, or absorbed by the state — “this displacement deepened the divide between rich and poor, fueling unrest.” That was Rome. But it could be any American veteran who came home from Iraq to a foreclosed house and a VA system that couldn’t see them for six months. The pattern is not metaphorical. It is structural. As I wrote then: “The struggles of inequality and civil liberties are not relics of the past, but persistent issues throughout every age.”

IV. The Persia Problem — Since the Greeks

There is a deeper pattern here, and it requires zooming out further than the seminar was willing to go. Western powers have been attempting to exert control over Persia for millennia. Since the Greeks. The comparison to Athens was raised in the room — one more day, the great state of Athens. But that comparison should be a warning, not a rhetorical flourish. Athens fell. The lesson of Athens is that great powers overextend and collapse. The follow-up question is: what are we doing to avoid that? The answer, based on current policy, is nothing. We are accelerating it.

The British drew the borders of the modern Middle East after World War I to serve imperial interests, not regional stability. In 1953, the CIA and MI6 orchestrated the overthrow of Iran’s democratically elected Prime Minister, Mohammad Mossadegh, to protect oil interests. We installed the Shah. The Shah fell. The Islamic Revolution followed. We backed Iraq against Iran through the 1980s. We butted in during the fifties and the seventies and we can’t deny it. We have not stayed out of the Middle East. It was us that bombed the school in Tehran. We have asked for this, and the East is going to bleed us dry.

Prussia came up in the seminar — how it attacked out of survival. I agree with that assessment. But that is precisely where I raise the issue: when survival becomes the justification for aggression, the line between defense and provocation disappears. We create our own problems. Every intervention generates the conditions for the next one. Every bombing creates the generation that will fight us next. This is not theory. This is observable history, repeating on a cycle that gets shorter and more expensive every time. In my art history coursework analyzing the Bayeux Tapestry (Moody, 2025b), I wrote that “the tapestry doesn’t present history neutrally; it performs history” — and that “the violence is part of the message: it communicates victory was earned, costly and decisive — exactly the kind of memory a new regime would want permanently stitched into the fabrics of history.” That was eleventh-century propaganda. Ours is the same mechanism with a different medium.

And the consequences are compounding. When Operation Epic Fury launched, strikes hit Tehran, Isfahan, Qom, Karaj, and Kermanshah. Iran retaliated across the entire Gulf — all U.S. bases hit simultaneously, a historic first. A school in Minab was struck, killing dozens of girls between the ages of seven and twelve. That is the image the rest of the world sees. Not liberation. Not strategy. Dead children in rubble. That image does not go away. And the people who survive it do not forget who sent the planes.

The war has now spread to at least twenty countries — shooting, shielding, or quietly supplying (Basu & Lawler, 2026). Multiple parties are shaping the conflict. It takes two to tango, but we insist on dancing alone. Every alliance, every unintended consequence, every retaliatory strike — these are not aberrations from the plan. There is no plan. There is only the pattern: intervene, escalate, declare victory, leave, repeat.

V. The Overextension — What the Data Shows

I am not speculating. I have been using a structured analytical framework since before the bombs fell. What it shows is this: the United States is overextended across multiple theaters with depleted reserves, and our adversaries know it.

The first six American casualties in this war were Iowa Army reservists from a logistics unit. Not active duty. Reservists. I predicted we would burn through reserve troops to fill gaps in force projection before it was confirmed in the reporting. Marines are deploying in force to the Pacific theater — confirmed through two independent sources, one active-duty, one adjacent — while the media covers Iran as if it’s the only theater that matters. China is watching. They are keeping their distance from the Iran conflict, and that is not neutrality. That is patience. They are watching us deplete resources, stretch supply lines across three theaters, and exhaust our force before they make their move.

What no one wants to pay attention to is what the rest of our adversaries are doing while we are focused on Iran. We are depleting our missile stockpiles. We have supply line and refueling problems that are not being discussed publicly. Our force is stretched across multiple theaters. Historically, every time the United States has been this committed in one region, adversaries have tested us in another. That is not a prediction — it is the documented pattern from Korea through the present. This is something we should be talking about, because this is how the war comes home. This is how it affects the people sitting in that auditorium.

Any strategist studying this from the outside would see the same thing: a stretched force with depleted reserves is an opportunity. That is not anti-American to say — it is basic military history, and it is exactly the kind of analysis taught in the courses at this institution. Our strategic vulnerability is not just material. It is psychological. There is an assumption built into American foreign policy that we will always be able to respond, that our capacity is limitless, that our position is unassailable. That assumption has never been tested at this scale, across this many theaters, with this level of domestic instability running underneath it.

And here is the detail that ties the history to the present in a way no one could make up: the Marines deploying to the Middle East are aboard the USS Tripoli — an amphibious assault ship named after the same Tripoli where the United States launched its first overseas military intervention in 1801. We are sending boots on the ground to the same region we have been intervening in since before we were fifty years old, on a ship named after the proof. The boots-on-the-ground escalation was predicted — both in the seminar and in my own analysis — since the campaign began. It was never a question of if. It was a question of which ship.

NATO will not intervene. The reporting makes this clear. The coalition stands at two nations openly: the United States and Israel. Some Gulf states provide varying degrees of tacit logistical support and basing access, but none have publicly committed forces or endorsed the campaign. The United Kingdom explicitly refused. Canada and Australia are calling for de-escalation. A U.S. drone strike hit Cyprus — a non-NATO country — and the alliance said nothing. We are acting as though the world is behind us when the world is watching us exhaust ourselves with one partner.

And it is worth pausing on that partnership, because the history matters. The modern state of Israel exists because the Western powers — primarily Britain and the United States — carved it out of Palestine in 1948. After the Holocaust, with millions of displaced European Jews seeking refuge, the international community faced a genuine moral crisis. The response was the UN Partition Plan: divide Palestine, give half to a new Jewish state, and tell the people already living there to make room. The Arab population rejected a plan they had no meaningful voice in shaping. War followed immediately. The United States recognized Israel within minutes of its declaration of independence. Britain, which had administered the territory under mandate, withdrew and left both sides to fight over borders that were drawn in conference rooms an ocean away. We helped create a nation, planted it in the middle of a region that did not want it there, armed it, funded it, and then spent the next seventy-eight years managing the consequences of that decision while acting surprised that the region remains unstable. The current war does not exist in a vacuum. It exists in the direct line of a choice made in 1948, compounded by every intervention since, and the coalition of two nations waging it is the same coalition that built the architecture this conflict runs on.

There is a line people repeat without thinking: the Middle East has been at war for thousands of years. We say it as though the region’s instability is indigenous — as though it exists in isolation from the civilizations that have been reaching into it since antiquity. We openly teach the Crusades. We recognize their significance. But we rarely follow the thread: Western influence is not a footnote in the region’s history of conflict. It is a through-line. The Crusades, the Ottoman partition, the Sykes-Picot Agreement, the British Mandate, the creation of Israel, the Mossadegh coup, the Gulf Wars, the War on Terror — the West has been drawing lines on that map for a thousand years and then expressing surprise when the people inside those lines fight over them. I am not saying Persia has never started a conflict. I am saying that the evolution of thought is there, in the historical record, for anyone willing to read it honestly — and I would rather lay out the facts as the history books say them now, before those books are rewritten to fit a narrative that serves the next war instead of preventing it. And consider this: Sykes-Picot carved up the Middle East in secret in 1916, redrawing borders behind closed doors without consulting the people who lived there. Thirty years later, we did the same thing to Europe openly — at Yalta, at Potsdam — and we call that one diplomacy. The method was the same. The only difference is that we were at the table for the second one. In 1916, they hid it because they knew it was wrong. In 1945, they did it at a press conference because winning made it feel justified. Same behavior, different confidence level. That is not strategy. That is what happens when victory validates the impulse instead of checking it.

Meanwhile, the Department of Homeland Security is leaderless and unfunded. Over one hundred thousand federal workers are unpaid during active combat operations across three theaters. Congress hasn’t convened an emergency session. The Senate met on Monday. The House waited until Wednesday. The war started on a Saturday — deliberately timed when markets were closed and Congress was out. This is wartime government failure. Basic state functions collapsing while military operations escalate. And nobody in that seminar mentioned any of it.

VI. The Domestic Mirror

In April 2024, I wrote a research paper titled “The Injustice of American Immigration” that examined the domestic expression of the same behavior we project abroad. The United States separates families at the border, detains asylum seekers without due process, and treats human beings as political leverage — all while claiming to be a beacon of freedom. The Trump administration’s “Zero Tolerance” policy separated over 4,600 children from their parents with no reunification plan. As of December 2024, at least 1,360 of those children had still never been reunited (Human Rights Watch, 2024). New separations continued — Al Otro Lado documented over 1,000 additional family separations in San Diego alone between September and December 2023 (UCLA Law). As of January 2026, 73,000 people were held in ICE detention — the highest number in ICE’s twenty-three-year history, an 84% increase from the year before. Of those detained, over half had no criminal conviction (CBS News/DHS). Approximately six thousand were family units — parents and children taken into custody together. Interior deportations have quadrupled since mid-2024, with ICE arrests increasing elevenfold for street operations alone (Deportation Data Project, 2025). We built a wall. We covered the Rio Grande in barbed wire. We bused migrants to cities we wanted to punish for being compassionate.

This is not a separate issue from the war in Iran. It is the same issue. The identity that bombs abroad is the same identity that dehumanizes at the border.

And the violence is not confined to the border. It is everywhere. On January 7, 2026, ICE agent Jonathan Ross shot and killed Renée Good — a 37-year-old American citizen, a writer, a mother — while she sat in her car in Minneapolis. Federal authorities claimed self-defense, stating Good attempted to ram the vehicle forward; witness accounts and a private autopsy dispute this characterization (NPR, 2026; Wikipedia). Eighteen days later, Border Patrol agents shot and killed Alex Pretti, a 37-year-old VA ICU nurse, during the protests that followed. Pretti was filming with his phone and trying to help a woman who had been pushed to the ground. He was pepper-sprayed, thrown down, and shot approximately ten times while surrounded by federal agents (PBS, 2026; Military.com, 2026). These are American citizens killed by their own government on American soil.

On July 13, 2024, a gunman shot then-candidate Donald Trump at a rally in Butler, Pennsylvania, grazing his ear and killing an audience member — Corey Comperatore, a volunteer firefighter who died shielding his family. On September 10, 2025, conservative activist Charlie Kirk was assassinated by a single shot while speaking at Utah Valley University. Whatever your politics, these are not normal events. A presidential candidate and a political commentator were both shot in the span of fourteen months. Kirk’s widow, Erika, was appointed by President Trump to the U.S. Air Force Academy’s Board of Visitors on March 10, 2026 — filling the seat her husband had been tapped for before his death (Fox News, 2026; Inside Higher Ed, 2026). I am not questioning her grief or her right to serve. I am pointing out that the pattern of political violence followed by political appointment is something we should be examining, not normalizing.

And then there is the matter no one in power wants to discuss honestly. The Epstein files. In July 2025, the Trump administration’s Department of Justice released a memo stating that Jeffrey Epstein’s “client list” did not exist and that “no credible evidence was found that Epstein blackmailed prominent individuals” (Axios, 2025). Congress disagreed, passed the Epstein Files Transparency Act, and forced the release of over 3.5 million documents in December 2025 and January 2026 (DOJ, 2026). What those documents revealed is still being processed, but what has already surfaced is this: at least half a dozen current administration officials appear in the files, including the Deputy Defense Secretary, the Secretary of the Navy, and the Secretary of Commerce (NBC News, 2026). The DOJ removed and withheld files specifically related to accusations about the sitting president (NPR, 2026). And in a detail that should make every parent in the country stop and think: the Wall Street Journal found that 43 of 47 victim names were left unredacted in the initial release, while the names of the powerful were protected. According to the Journal’s review, the government exposed the victims and shielded the accused. School shootings may be declining — and that is genuinely good news — but an administration that claims to protect children while its own officials appear in the files of the most prolific child sex trafficker in modern American history is an administration whose stated values deserve scrutiny. The reader can decide what that means. I am simply pointing out that the data exists.

None of this is okay. Not the shootings, not the assassinations, not the retaliatory appointments, not the federal agents killing citizens at protests. Every one of these events is a warning — not of a single ideology gone wrong, but of a society in which narrative has replaced reason, grievance has replaced governance, and violence has become the loudest form of political speech. And this is not unique to one administration. Obama expanded the drone program that normalized extrajudicial killing abroad. Biden continued border enforcement policies his own party had condemned. The machinery of overreach is bipartisan; the current administration is operating it at higher speed, but they did not build it alone. That is the domestic mirror of the war abroad. The decay is not coming. It is here.

In my criminology research on the suicidality of mass shooters (Moody, 2025c), I examined how labeling theory operates at the individual level — how “constant media coverage does not just inform the public; it can amplify fear, normalize extreme narratives, and accelerate the cycle of violence.” When you label someone a terrorist, a criminal, an outsider, they either resist the label or conform to it. The same mechanism applies to communities, to demographics, and to nations. We label our enemies. We label our own citizens. And the labels create the behavior we claimed to be protecting against. I also analyzed how media framing constructs reality (Moody, 2025d) — how the same violent event can be presented as an isolated crime or as a symptom of systemic failure, depending on who controls the narrative. The framing is a form of power. And right now, the framing around this war is being controlled by the people waging it.

There is a concept in criminology called labeling theory — once a person or group is labeled, they tend to conform to the label. Apply that at a national scale. The United States has labeled itself the toughest nation in the room. The strongest. The most righteous. And that label demands performance. It demands that we act like the toughest, which means we cannot back down, we cannot admit failure, and we cannot stop fighting — because stopping would mean we aren’t who we say we are. That is the ooh-rah mentality at its core — a subtle, militaristic worldview that says we have to be the bully, because that is what we have been told we are, and the label does not change unless we choose to change it.

Here is the irony that no one in that seminar addressed: introspection is only painful at first. Every therapist in the country knows this. The results come when the subject stops resisting the process and applies the material. Behavioral change follows self-examination — not the other way around. Our country trains psychologists, social workers, counselors, and behavioral scientists specifically to guide people through this process. We fund entire university departments dedicated to it. We credential people to do it. And then, at the national level, we ignore them when their conclusions are inconvenient. We train people to train people to look inward — and then we refuse to look inward ourselves. The tools exist. The expertise exists. The discipline that could help us break this cycle is the same discipline we underfund, dismiss, and override with rhetoric every time the conversation gets uncomfortable. We refuse to take these lessons, to look inward, naturally out of fear — fear of being exposed, fear of being told we are wrong. Nobody likes to hear it. But enabling the behavior does not make it go away. It makes it worse. Every clinician in this building knows that.

Psychologically, that kind of pressure causes people to lash out. It is the behavior of someone who knows their position is slipping and compensates with aggression rather than adaptation. At a national level, this is what we are watching. We are losing our standing as the uncontested superpower. We know it. And the response — authoritarian consolidation at home, unilateral military action abroad, the erosion of legislative checks — these are not the actions of a confident democracy. VII. The Erosion We’re Not Talking About

Peter Leyden, in a Big Think interview that has since reached millions of viewers, laid out the framework: the next twenty-five years will force humanity to reinvent itself (Leyden, 2025). American politics oscillates in roughly twenty-five-year cycles. McCarthy to Kennedy. The seventies to Reagan. The nineties to the War on Terror. Each cycle features a consolidation of power followed by a democratic correction. But the cycles are compressing, and there is a new variable that no prior generation has contended with: algorithmic speed.

The democratic correction mechanism requires time. Courts take months. Elections take years. Congressional votes take weeks. The cascades I have been tracking are moving in hours and days. War was authorized without Congress, and then Congress debated it after the fact. The constitutional checks are not functioning at the speed required to constrain executive action in an era where the information environment is synthetic, operations are kinetic before the public knows they’ve started, and AI systems are being conscripted into the war effort before anyone votes on whether they should be.

On February 27th, Anthropic — an AI company that builds large language models — refused Pentagon demands to drop safety guardrails on their systems. Within hours, the Secretary of Defense designated them a “supply chain risk to national security.” That same day, OpenAI — which had quietly deleted its military use prohibition in 2024 — signed the Pentagon contract. The government blacklisted the company that said no, rewarded the company that said yes, and went to war the next morning. That is not a footnote. That is the infrastructure of the next era being built in real time, and it is being built to comply.

Each of these events — the war, the AI conscription, the congressional paralysis, the alliance fractures, the reserve depletion, the domestic crackdowns — gets reported as a separate story. Nobody is mapping them as a single system. But they are a single system, and every thread feeds into the same outcome: the compression of democratic capacity to constrain executive action, accelerated by algorithmic speed, in an increasingly synthetic information environment. Democratic erosion is invisible because it’s the water, not the fish. By the time you notice you’re drowning, the surface is already above you.

VIII. The Responsibility of the Room

The role of educators in this moment matters. If historians and political scientists stand at a podium and walk a room through the pattern without naming it as a pattern — without saying plainly that we have been doing the same thing for seventy years and it has not worked — then the institution is failing at its most basic function. We are supposed to be training the next generation to think critically about power, policy, and consequence. “Well, we’re at war again, but that’s just what we do” is not analysis. It is surrender dressed in academic neutrality.

In January 2026, I wrote an essay called “The Moral Bankruptcy of the Objective Observer” — not for a class, but because I couldn’t stop thinking about it. The central argument is this: in our current era, the most profound tragedy is not the overwhelming noise generated by the uninformed, but rather the calculated silence of those who possess deep knowledge. When individuals equipped with expertise in history, psychology, and sociology choose to remain neutral during times of systemic collapse, they are not practicing objectivity. They are engaging in what Bandura (1999) calls moral disengagement — a fundamental betrayal of the principle that knowledge should serve humanity. To have the ability to understand human suffering and yet refuse to act because of “timing” or awaiting a “grant” is not caution. It is cowardice dressed in procedure.

Frankl’s logotherapy teaches that the search for meaning is the primary drive of human existence. For scholars, meaning is found in the pursuit of truth. But if truth is divorced from its application to protecting people, it becomes mere vanity. When academia treats the uncertainty of the future as a topic to be studied rather than a crisis to be addressed, the ivory tower transforms into a bunker — hoarding data while the very subjects of that data are left isolated and vulnerable. Arendt’s concept of the banality of evil illustrates that the greatest harms often originate not from malicious actors, but from those who rigidly follow protocol and ignore the consequences. In contemporary academia, this manifests as a culture that values institutional safety and tenure above the ethical duty to defend the vulnerable. If research on trauma, displacement, or inequality does not motivate scholars to act, then such research fails its most basic moral test.

There is a structural empathy gap at work here. Using Maslow’s hierarchy as a framework, those who have achieved self-actualization and tenure security are fundamentally disconnected from the communities they study — communities still focused on basic safety and survival. And the bystander effect operates at an institutional scale: the belief that someone else with more authority or a larger platform will speak up is a collective delusion that ensures no one does. That silence provides moral cover for the erosion of constitutional integrity, granting what amounts to a sanction of victimhood — extending the logic of Latané and Darley’s bystander effect to the institutional level — signaling to the public that their suffering is not significant enough to warrant intervention.

Freire wrote that the academic vocation is a kind of secular priesthood dedicated to enlightening society. Witnessing metaphorical — or literal — book burnings and choosing career stability over defending the marginalized is an act of intellectual cowardice. True scholarship requires the courage to move from observation to assertive advocacy. We do not need more papers on collapse. We need the authors of those papers to rise. The question is whether academics will be remembered as the librarians of decline, or as the architects of resilience.

And the problem is not limited to what is said from podiums. It is built into the programs themselves. There are degree programs I have become shocked to learn do not require a psychology component. Creative writing is one of them — but they have history in their curriculum. How are we going to teach about history without analyzing the behaviors of those who implemented the policy changes before us, all the way back to Sumeria, when the first recorded laws were stamped in clay? How do we brush over the critical analysis skills needed to understand why societies want change, yet for the majority of history ideas took hundreds of years to light a fire in someone enough to exact the appropriate change? The lessons from history are here to be analyzed. The tools to teach those analytical skills exist. They are not being deployed at scale. If we cannot connect the study of human behavior to the study of human history inside the institutions designed to do exactly that, then we are not educating. We are credentialing.

I want to be clear: I am not accusing faculty of cowardice. I understand that professors within the SUNY system face real institutional constraints on what they can say from a podium. Faculty I trust have spoken to me privately about the fear of government repercussion at public institutions — and that fear is not hypothetical. There is a difference between choosing silence out of comfort and being silenced by a system that punishes dissent. Many of the people I respect most in these hallways are in the second category, not the first. They are doing what they can within the space they are allowed. But that is exactly the problem the essay is naming: when the space for honest analysis shrinks to the point where only students can say what professors think, the institution has already failed at its stated mission. I am a student. And I believe it is not only my right but my responsibility to say what I see — not to perform outrage, not to wave a flag, not to parrot a political line, but to take the knowledge I have spent years building and apply it honestly.

And to defend the professors further — what is happening to them is not abstract. The raids on Columbia. The crackdowns at Harvard. Students removed, sidelined, or deported not for committing crimes but for holding opinions. Regardless of nationality, regardless of which side of the debate they stood on, removing people from academic institutions because of a difference of opinion is a violation of the principles this country was built on. It is hypocritical. And it is dangerous — not because of what those students said, but because of what it tells every student and every professor who watches it happen: stay quiet, or you are next.

The free exchange of ideas is not a luxury. It is the engine. The STEM programs born out of the 1950s — fueled by the urgency of the space race, by the Cold War competition that said we had to out-think the Soviets, not just out-fight them — those programs produced the scientists, the engineers, the thinkers who built the world we live in now. The thoughts I myself have were shaped by that legacy. The interdisciplinary thinking this essay attempts was made possible by an educational infrastructure that was built to compete with ideas, not suppress them. We made our greatest progress when we fought with science. We made our worst decisions when we stopped listening to it. The flow of fresh ideas — uncomfortable ideas, dissenting ideas, ideas that challenge the people in power — is what propelled this country through the Cold War, through the space race, through every era in which we actually led by example instead of just claiming to. That flow is what makes us American. Cutting it off to protect a narrative is not strength. It is the beginning of the decline the narrative was supposed to prevent.

And it is not hypothetical. It is happening right now. The Department of Education — the institutional descendant of the same National Defense Education Act that responded to Sputnik by investing over a billion dollars in American minds — is being gutted. Staffing has been cut in half. Over $6 billion in approved education grants have been withheld from schools, including $1.4 billion for before- and after-school programs and $1.3 billion for STEM and college counseling (NPR, 2025; Chalkbeat, 2025). Eighteen programs under the Every Student Succeeds Act are being consolidated with $4.5 billion fewer dollars — a 70% reduction. Title III funding for English learners has been eliminated entirely. The administration’s own budget proposal cuts the Department by $12 billion, or 15.3% (NPR, 2025). The same country that built its dominance by out-thinking the Soviets is now defunding the infrastructure that made the thinking possible. The impact of that decision has not been felt yet. It will be. And when it is, the people who made it will not be the ones paying for it. The students will.

IX. Globalization Is Not Optional

There is a broader context that the seminar did not touch, and it is the one that makes everything else worse: globalization. In a criminology course last semester, I wrote that globalization theory generally holds that statistically, we are getting better as a whole — but as always in statistics, there are outliers. Hate crimes. Regional conflicts. Systemic failures. The way to heal those wounds is through education and rehabilitation, not through force projection. That position hasn’t changed.

In a philosophy course (Moody, 2025e), I wrote that “if there were no countries, then the insurmountable ideas of who’s right and wrong would diminish” — and in the same breath admitted that “my true analysis is we’re probably going to destroy ourselves sooner rather than later.” That was March 2025. I wish I had been wrong.

What has changed is the speed. Globalization is not a policy choice. It is an inevitability. Technology, trade, information, culture — they do not respect borders, and no amount of tariffs or walls or military posturing will reverse the interconnection of the modern world. The question has never been whether globalization happens. The question is whether we manage it with intelligence and humility, or whether we fight it with the same imperial instincts that got us here.

The war in Iran is, among other things, an attempt to fight globalization with bullets. The Strait of Hormuz is not just a military chokepoint — it is a global economic artery. Disrupt it, and the consequences cascade across every continent. Oil prices, shipping lanes, food supply chains, energy markets. The interconnection that globalization built is the same interconnection that makes this war everyone’s problem. We cannot bomb our way to a world that runs on our terms anymore, because the world doesn’t run on anyone’s terms. It runs on systems, and systems don’t respond to threats. They respond to disruption by failing.

X. The Rhythm

I cannot say for certain what comes next. What I can say is this: judging by the trajectory of every empire ever created, and how fast information spreads, and how technology accelerates that spread faster and faster, each accompanying conflict — from regional skirmish to global war — can be traced back to this rhythm. And this war is no different.

Jared Diamond demonstrated in Guns, Germs, and Steel that civilizations do not rise because they are inherently superior. They rise because of geography, resources, and environmental advantage. The Europeans who colonized the Americas did not do so because they were smarter or braver. They did it because they had steel, horses, and diseases their hosts had no immunity to. The myth of inherent superiority is what every dominant civilization tells itself after the fact — and it is the same myth that drives the lashing out when dominance starts to slip. This is the overarching thesis: every pillar of civilization that has reached the top of the heap has done the same thing. They knew better, and they lashed out anyway.

The Crusades were Europe lashing out eastward under religious justification — nine campaigns over two centuries that accomplished nothing permanent except the transfer of knowledge back from the Islamic world, which ironically fueled the Renaissance. The Renaissance produced the science and navigation that enabled colonialism. Colonialism produced the resource extraction that enabled the Industrial Revolution. The Industrial Revolution produced the military capacity that enabled two world wars. And the world wars produced the American century — which is now producing the war in Iran. Each cycle feeds the next. Each one is shorter than the last. And each one is accompanied by a technological leap that compresses the timeline further. The printing press, the telegraph, the radio, the television, the internet, and now algorithmic AI — each one accelerated the speed at which information moves, which accelerates the speed at which conflicts escalate, which shrinks the window for democratic correction every single time.

The roughly twenty-five-year cycle that keeps appearing in American politics is not unique to America. It is the civilizational rhythm. McCarthy to Kennedy. The seventies to Reagan. The nineties to the War on Terror. The War on Terror to now. Each cycle features consolidation, overreach, correction. But the correction window is compressing because technology is compressing it, and the people running the show either do not see it or do not care. Diamond’s entire argument is that civilizations collapse not because they lack resources but because they make catastrophic decisions about how to use them. We are watching that in real time.

There is an evolutionary framework that explains why this keeps happening, and it is not metaphorical. David Sloan Wilson’s multilevel selection theory holds that natural selection operates simultaneously at the level of individuals and groups. Groups that cooperate outcompete groups that do not — that is how democracies, alliances, and civilizations rise. But within those groups, selfish actors outcompete cooperative ones — that is how they fall. America rose because group-level cooperation worked: collective defense, shared institutions, constitutional checks, allied coalitions. What we are watching now is the within-group dynamic taking over. Power consolidation, institutional erosion, the labeling and exclusion of dissent — these are the selfish strategies that outcompete cooperation in the short term and destroy the group in the long term. And at the global scale, we are defecting from the cooperative system that made us dominant in the first place. Pulling out of alliances, fighting globalization, blacklisting companies that say no. Evolution does not care about national pride. The math is simple: groups that stop cooperating get outcompeted by groups that don’t.

They get costlier every time. Not just in lives, but in things people don’t think about: public opinion, war fatigue, the psychological toll on a population that has been at war for the entirety of its living memory. People get tired of war within a generation or two. Consistently. This will technically be the third war I’ve witnessed in my lifetime, and I genuinely hope I make it through this one. Because we have pissed the world off enough, and stuffed our faces where it probably wasn’t the best idea, for so long, that this actually tracks. This is not surprising. It is the logical consequence of seventy years of behavior.

Bombing creates the disdain that fuels the next conflict. We have the data. We have centuries of it. The cycle is the problem, and treating each war as a discrete event disconnected from the last is how we keep ending up here. If the objective is to topple Iran, then what is the objective after that? If there isn’t one, then we are not waging war. We are performing it.

XI. Where This Goes

I am not going to predict what happens next. I have done that already, in writing, and scored myself against the outcomes. What I will do is lay out what has already happened, what is happening now, and what the pattern says follows — based on the same historical precedents I have studied in the courses I passed at this institution, taught by its professors, using the analytical methods they graded me on.

What has already happened: Operation Epic Fury is active across multiple countries. The Strait of Hormuz has confirmed mine deployments — mines that persist after hostilities end and require weeks to months of clearance. Oil spiked above $119 per barrel. Over 700,000 Lebanese have been displaced in under two weeks. The first American casualties were reserve troops from a logistics unit in Iowa. Congress has not convened an emergency session. Russia is confirmed sharing intelligence with Iran during active combat. The WHO has issued a “black rain” warning from the bombing of Iranian oil facilities. These are not projections. These are facts, reported across multiple outlets in the feed sources cited at the end of this paper.

What is happening now: Gulf allies are reporting critical interceptor shortages. They are requesting U.S. resupply, but we are conducting offensive operations while our allies conduct defensive operations, both drawing from the same munitions supply chain. That math does not work. China is consolidating internally — Hong Kong press freedom is deteriorating, and public sentiment is being shaped to frame any future action as anti-American rather than anti-Taiwanese. North Korea is testing cruise missiles from a new naval destroyer during an active conflict. Cuba is experiencing rolling blackouts and acute food shortages while the U.S. is committed elsewhere. None of this is classified. It is all in the open-source reporting.

What the pattern says follows: Every time the United States has overextended militarily while neglecting domestic stability, adversaries have exploited the gap. That is not a prediction. That is what happened after Korea, after Vietnam, after Iraq, and after Afghanistan. The question is not whether it happens again. The question is whether we recognize it in time to do something about it. Every geographic expansion of a conflict multiplies the actors, the refugees, and the political complexity. The “over by Christmas” pattern from World War I is not a metaphor — it is a documented failure of timeline assumptions during wartime that has repeated in every major American conflict since. Meanwhile, the administration’s own narrative is contradicting itself: claiming “rapidly declining” Iranian capability while casualties mount, declaring Cuba “ready to fall” while Cuba files terrorism charges against the United States, and dismissing homeland attack risk while the Department of Homeland Security remains leaderless and unfunded.

The through-line is democratic erosion. Not through a dramatic coup, but through the slow compression of the public’s ability to check executive power. War without congressional authorization. AI companies blacklisted for refusing to comply. Allies depleted and ignored. Environmental consequences that outlast any ceasefire. And an information environment moving faster than the institutions designed to govern it. This is not where I think it goes. This is where it has always gone, documented across the same historical record that I was taught to read in the classrooms down the hall.

XII. The Strongest Case for This War — and Why It Still Doesn’t Hold

If this essay is going to be honest, it has to engage with the best argument in favor of what we are doing. And the best argument is simple: prevent another Pearl Harbor. Full stop.

That is not a frivolous argument. It is rooted in genuine national trauma — a wound that has shaped American foreign policy for eighty-five years and counting. On December 7, 1941, the United States learned what happens when you let a threat develop unchecked. 2,403 Americans died in a single morning because we did not act preemptively. The lesson carved into the national psyche was: never again. Hit them before they hit us. The preemptive strike doctrine that has defined every military engagement since — from Korea to Iraq to Iran — flows directly from that morning in Hawaii.

But the trauma goes deeper than Pearl Harbor. It goes back to 1814, when the British marched into Washington, burned the White House, burned the Capitol, and left. They didn’t stay. They didn’t occupy. They made their point and sailed away. And that humiliation — the fact that a foreign power reached the seat of American government and set it on fire — has never fully healed. We won the Revolutionary War, defeated the greatest empire on earth, declared ourselves a new kind of nation — and then thirty years later, that same empire walked into our capital and burned it down. The identity wound from that moment is still load-bearing in the national psyche. We have to be the strongest. We have to be untouchable. Because the alternative is 1814 again.

You can trace the anxiety forward: 1814 to the Monroe Doctrine. The Monroe Doctrine to Manifest Destiny. Pearl Harbor to the permanent security state. September 11th to the forever wars. Each time we get hit, the response is not proportional — it is existential. We do not respond to attacks. We respond to the idea that we are attackable. And the response is always the same: project force everywhere, so no one ever reaches us again.

The fear is real. I am not dismissing it. But the pattern it produces is the problem.

On February 25, 1942 — less than three months after Pearl Harbor — the city of Los Angeles erupted in panic. Anti-aircraft batteries across the coast opened fire on the night sky. Over 1,400 rounds of anti-aircraft ammunition were fired into the darkness above the city. Air raid sirens wailed. A citywide blackout was ordered. The population was told they were under attack. They were not. There was no enemy aircraft. The best evidence suggests the initial radar contacts were weather balloons or possibly stray meteorological equipment. The military fired at nothing for hours, and five civilians died — three from car accidents in the blackout and two from heart attacks caused by the stress. This was the Battle of Los Angeles, and it was not a battle. It was a trauma response at a national scale.

That is what Pearl Harbor did to us. Not just the military reorganization, not just the intelligence reforms — it made us see threats in empty skies. And we have been firing at weather balloons ever since. The difference is that now the weather balloons are sovereign nations, and the rounds are Tomahawk missiles, and the civilians dying are not in Los Angeles. They are in Tehran and Beirut and the schoolyards of Minab.

The preemptive strike doctrine says: we must act before the threat materializes, because the cost of waiting is Pearl Harbor. But here is what the doctrine does not account for: every preemptive strike creates the next threat. We overthrew Mossadegh in 1953 to prevent Soviet influence in Iran. That overthrow produced the 1979 revolution. The revolution produced the hostage crisis. The hostage crisis produced forty-five years of mutual hostility. And that hostility produced the war we are fighting right now. The threat we are preempting in 2026 is the threat we created in 1953. The doctrine is eating its own tail.

So yes — the strongest case for this war is fear. Fear of being hit. Fear of appearing weak. Fear of another Pearl Harbor, another 9/11, another morning where the world changes before breakfast. That fear is human. It is understandable. And it is exactly the kind of fear that, in an individual, a psychologist would diagnose as hypervigilance — a hallmark of post-traumatic stress. The nation is not calculating. It is reacting. And the reaction, every single time, makes the next reaction inevitable.

I watched the towers come down. I am not dismissing what that kind of attack does to a nation. The retaliation — going into Afghanistan, dismantling al-Qaeda’s operational capacity — was justified. Sending troops to defend freedom in the immediate aftermath of September 11th was the right call. But what came after was not retaliation. It was mission creep dressed in the language of the original wound. The oil contracts, the Halliburton deals, the indefinite occupation, the pivot to Iraq on intelligence we knew was shaky — that was not defense. That was the good decision becoming a bad one while nobody was willing to say so, because questioning the war meant questioning the grief, and nobody wanted to be the one who dishonored the dead by asking whether we were honoring them with the right strategy.

And then there is the bin Laden raid. On May 2, 2011, the United States sent helicopters and special operations forces into Abbottabad, Pakistan — a sovereign, nuclear-armed ally — without that government’s knowledge or permission. One of the Black Hawks crashed in the compound. If that raid had gone wrong — if Pakistani military had responded, if it had become a firefight with a nuclear power — we would be having a very different conversation right now. We do not talk about that risk because the mission succeeded. But the risk was real, and it was the kind of sovereignty violation we would call an act of war if someone did it to us. History is written by outcomes, not by the odds we beat to get there.

The pattern is always the same: a justified response escalates into something unjustifiable, and by the time anyone notices, the original justification has become a shield against accountability. We are watching it happen again in Iran. This war could have been approached with diplomacy. It could have been a coordinated multilateral effort with clear objectives and international legitimacy. Instead, we struck first and are asking questions later — and now we are experiencing what happens when you do that.

I understand striking when the opportunity arises. I understand that there are bad actors who need to be confronted. But if we claim to lead by example, then we are supposed to lead by example: exercise restraint, build coalitions, ground our actions in verifiable intelligence rather than rhetoric. When the narrative can be spun so many ways that fact becomes indistinguishable from emotionally driven reaction, we have lost the moral authority that was supposed to justify the action in the first place. A country that leads by example does not need to explain why the example looks like belligerence.

The strongest case for this war is the same case that has been made for every war since 1941. And every time we have accepted it, we have ended up exactly where we are now — overextended, exhausted, with more enemies than we started with, wondering how we got here again. At some point, the pattern is the answer.

And I refuse to sit in a room where the pattern is laid out in plain sight and pretend — along with everyone else — that it doesn’t lead exactly where it has always led.

While we are still alive and still capable of choosing differently, we should be looking inward. We should be informing each other. We should be promoting the kind of interdisciplinary work that connects the dots between history and psychology and policy and data — the kind of work this paper attempts to do — instead of retreating into silos and waiting for the next headline to tell us what to think. If we haven’t all killed each other by the end of the year, that would be a start.

This is the world we have to live with. Not the one we were promised, not the one we imagined, but the one that exists right now — shaped by every decision documented in this paper and the centuries of decisions that preceded them. The current situation is going to get worse. I do not know exactly how, and I will not pretend to. But I can guess, based on the patterns I have studied, and every guess I have made so far has landed closer to reality than I wanted it to. Korea, Vietnam, and Afghanistan were not enough of a lesson. The question is whether we are willing to learn from this one.

We have every tool at our disposal to change course. We have the institutions, the knowledge, the economic leverage, the diplomatic infrastructure, and the historical record. The information is not hidden. It is in the open-source reporting that anyone with a news feed and a willingness to read can see. The problem is not access. The problem is that somewhere along the way, we decided that the loudest voice in the room is the strongest one, and we stopped listening to anything quieter than a threat.
